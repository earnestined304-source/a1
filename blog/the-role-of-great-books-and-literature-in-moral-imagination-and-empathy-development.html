Literature, Ethics & Character

The Role of Great Books and Literature in Moral Imagination and Empathy Development

✍️ Dr. Beatrice Montgomery, Chair of Comparative Literature & Ethics 📅 July 29, 2026 ⏱️ 12 min read

In an era increasingly shaped by brief digital snippets, utilitarian career preparation, and algorithmic content curation, the deep reading of classic literature is frequently marginalized as an ornamental luxury. Yet, throughout civilizational history, the serious encounter with Great Books has served as the indispensable crucible for the formation of the moral imagination. Engaging with the monumental literary and philosophical works of human history does not merely improve vocabulary; it fundamentally expands the boundaries of the human soul, allowing young scholars to experience the complexities, tragedies, and transcendent nobility of the human condition across millennia.

📚

The Concept of Moral Imagination

As coined by Edmund Burke and articulated by Russell Kirk, the moral imagination is the human capacity to perceive ethical truth, human dignity, and enduring virtue beyond the narrow boundaries of immediate physical experience and temporal circumstance.

1. Literature as the Laboratory of Ethical Experience

Abstract ethical codes and moral imperatives, when presented purely as didactic rules, often fail to move the human heart. A child can memorize a list of virtues—courage, justice, temperance, prudence—yet remain completely unequipped to navigate the murky, agonizing moral dilemmas of actual life.

Great literature functions as an ethical simulator. When a student reads Sophocles' Antigone, they do not merely absorb an ancient plot; they feel the visceral tension between the sacred duty owed to familial conscience and the civic allegiance demanded by the state. When they accompany Prince Andrei in Tolstoy's War and Peace as he lies wounded upon the battlefield of Austerlitz gazing into the infinite sky, they confront their own mortality and the vanity of ambition.

2. Theory of Mind and Cognitive Empathy in Literary Reading

Modern cognitive psychology and neuroimaging provide profound empirical backing for the transformative power of deep narrative reading. Landmark studies conducted by neuroscientists demonstrate that reading literary fiction—as opposed to popular genre fiction or informational non-fiction—uniquely activates the neural networks underlying Theory of Mind.

Theory of Mind is the cognitive capacity to attribute mental states—beliefs, intents, desires, emotions, and knowledge—to oneself and others, understanding that others have beliefs and desires distinct from one's own.

3. The Great Conversation Across Millennia

At WisdomSchool, literature is not taught in disjointed thematic units or modern anthologies. Instead, students enter what Robert Maynard Hutchins called The Great Conversation: the ongoing dialogue between the master thinkers, poets, dramatists, and philosophers of human history.

Every major classic responds to, challenges, or refines its predecessors:

  • Virgil reads Homer and recasts the martial rage of Achilles into the dutiful, sorrowful civic piety of Aeneas.
  • Dante reads Virgil and elevates the classical Roman journey through the underworld into a cosmic pilgrimage toward divine love and redemption.
  • Milton reads Dante and Virgil, re-imagining the Fall of humanity in Paradise Lost with breathtaking psychological complexity.
  • Mary Shelley reads Milton, crafting Frankenstein as an enduring critique of unbridled hubristic rationalism untethered from moral responsibility.

4. Character Formation and the Rescue from Temporal Provincialism

One of the gravest dangers of modern education is chronological snobbery—the uncritical assumption that the ideas, fashions, and values of the current decade are inherently superior to those of all previous centuries.

Reading Great Books rescues young minds from this temporal provincialism. It introduces them to civilizations that possessed radically different virtues, flaws, and worldviews, instantly humbling their intellectual pride. A student who has read Thucydides' History of the Peloponnesian War cannot be easily deceived by modern political propaganda; they have already witnessed the demagoguery of Cleon and the tragic hubris of the Melian Dialogue.

"To read great literature is to enjoy the company of the wisest, most sorrowful, and most joyful minds that ever lived. A person who reads only contemporary works is like a traveler who never leaves their native village."

— Dr. Beatrice Montgomery, The Architecture of Moral Imagination

5. The Pedagogical Practice: Socratic Dialectic Over Dogmatic Analysis

How literature is taught matters as much as what is read. If a teacher treats Shakespeare's Macbeth as a puzzle where students merely hunt for metaphors to pass an exam, the moral power of the drama is neutered.

At WisdomSchool, we explore Great Books through open Socratic inquiry that forces students to confront the tragic consequences of moral choices:

Seminar Facilitator: "In Act I of Macbeth, Macbeth acknowledges that his ambition has 'no spur to prick the sides of my intent, but only vaulting ambition.' Why does a man who clearly recognizes the moral horror of his intended deed proceed anyway?"

Student A: "Because Lady Macbeth questions his manhood, and his pride cannot endure being seen as cowardly."

Student B: "It is deeper than pride—Macbeth believes he can control the future, forgetting that an unjust act unleashes consequences that destroy the perpetrator's peace forever."

6. The Hermeneutics of Translation: Comparative Linguistic Analysis

A distinctive element of literature study at WisdomSchool is comparative translation analysis. When reading Homer's Iliad, Dante's Inferno, or Tolstoy's Anna Karenina, scholars do not rely upon a single modern translation.

Instead, students compare multiple translations alongside the original text:

  • Comparing Alexander Pope's rhymed heroic couplets with Richmond Lattimore's strict dactylic hexameter and Robert Fagles' dynamic modern verse.
  • Analyzing how word choices shape philosophical meaning—such as the difference between translating the Greek arete as 'excellence' versus 'virtue'.
  • Fostering linguistic humility by recognizing the subtle dimensions of poetic nuance that inevitably resist simple direct translation.

7. The Socratic Salon: Intergenerational Great Books Dialogues

At WisdomSchool, the study of literature is not confined within classroom walls. Once each month, scholars host the Evening Socratic Salon, welcoming parents, alumni, and visiting scholars to join the seminar table.

Together, teenagers and seasoned adults deliberate upon enduring texts such as Dostoevsky's The Grand Inquisitor or Augustine's Confessions. This intergenerational dialogue creates an authentic community of lifelong learners, demonstrating that the pursuit of wisdom is a perpetual, noble human journey.

8. Cultivating the Humane Soul for a Technological Century

As society hurtles into an increasingly mechanized future governed by artificial systems and data metrics, the preservation of the humane soul becomes the preeminent moral challenge of education.

  • Protection of Aesthetic Sensibility: Cultivating the capacity to recognize and be moved by genuine beauty, poetry, and linguistic harmony.
  • Strengthening Moral Discernment: Developing the ethical compass needed to guide technological power with humane wisdom.
  • Universal Human Solidarity: Discovering that beneath differences of language, epoch, and culture, the essential longings of the human heart for truth, justice, love, and reconciliation remain eternal.

The Living Word

The Great Books are not dead artifacts gathering dust in museum archives; they are living fountains of wisdom. By inviting our scholars into this timeless conversation, WisdomSchool ensures that our graduates enter the world not merely as technically proficient workers, but as deeply empathetic, morally courageous citizens of the world.
